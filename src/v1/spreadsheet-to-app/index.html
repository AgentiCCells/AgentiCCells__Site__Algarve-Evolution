Spreadsheet to App

Bring a spreadsheet from your work, leave with an app.

A programme of sessions proposed to us by the SGit Newsroom on 31 October 2026. One premise: let's turn your spreadsheet into an application. It would bring out the people we most want to reach: people in local businesses who already run part of their work on spreadsheets, who are curious about AI, and who want to understand it by doing something useful.

Try it now, in your browserRead the five prompts

Source: SGit Newsroom

The only two requirements

  1. An account with a model that can build an app: Lovable, Claude, ChatGPT, Mistral or another.
  2. A spreadsheet or document from your work, with dummy data: the same shape as the real one, close enough to be useful, and safe to publish.

That is all. Dummy data keeps everything confidential out, so nothing has to be protected, and it means the app can be published and shared on the day, which is where the learning happens.

Bring a laptop and a charger. Free tiers can work; event credits help.

Try it

No blank page

Most workshops stall at the same moment: someone asks what problem you want to solve, and the room goes quiet. Here, people arrive with the problem already written down, as a spreadsheet they use every week. For anyone who arrives without one, there are five ready-made examples, invented and publishable. Pick one, or bring your own, and watch the five prompts turn it into an app.

This page builds the app with simple rules in your browser, not with an AI model, so you can see in seconds the kind of app the five prompts produce. On the day, you build your own with the model you choose.

Pay per use, simulated: every action in the tool costs a few cents of credit (watch the counter at the bottom left). Nothing is charged; the credit lives only in this browser.

The method

Five prompts, in order

Use any model that can build and publish a small web app: Lovable, Claude, ChatGPT, Mistral or another. Paste your spreadsheet (dummy data only) or one of the examples with the first prompt, then send the others one at a time, using the app between each.

  1. 1

    Prompt 1

    A list I can search

    Here is my spreadsheet, with dummy data. Build a single-page web app that shows it as a list I can search and filter. Keep the data in the browser: no database, no login, no server. Show the columns that matter most first.
  2. 2

    Prompt 2

    Add and change rows

    Add a form to add a row and to edit or delete one. Save everything in the browser's local storage, and add buttons to export the data as CSV and to import it again.
  3. 3

    Prompt 3

    The view I use most

    The view I use most is: [today's tasks / what to order / unpaid invoices / this week's lessons / members who have not paid]. Make it the first screen, with the most important items first, and let me mark each one done.
  4. 4

    Prompt 4

    Remember every run

    Every time I finish a run (a day, an order, a session), keep a small dated record of what I did, what I skipped and a note. Show the history as a list, newest first.
  5. 5

    Prompt 5

    Publish it and say what it is

    Publish it so someone else can open it. Write a short "about" section: what the app does, where the data is kept, and what it does not do yet.

After the five: use it for real, then come back with what broke or what you missed. That is the next prompt.

A mock event (simulated)

The day

A one-day session, in the format of the Vibathon Algarve 2026. Mentors at the tables, a moment where each app is handed to someone at another table, and a session on what it takes for five or fifty people to use it.

Source: SGit Newsroom

  1. 09:00Welcome; why spreadsheets become apps now (15 min)
  2. 09:15Demo: one example spreadsheet to a published app, live, with the five prompts (30 min)
  3. 09:45Build, part 1: prompts 1 and 2, with mentors at the tables
  4. 11:30Show one thing that worked and one that broke (two minutes per table)
  5. 12:00Lunch
  6. 13:00Build, part 2: prompts 3, 4 and 5; publish
  7. 15:30Share: give your app to someone at another table and watch them use it
  8. 16:00The handover: what it takes for five or fifty people to use it (backups, changes, security)
  9. 16:45Show and tell; the jury's picks
  10. 17:30Next steps: who will release which apps properly, and how

Where the apps live

Three ways to publish

A programme can use all three.

  1. A

    Each person's own tool

    Lovable, a Claude artifact or a ChatGPT canvas can all publish a small app with a link.

  2. B

    A GitHub organisation for the programme

    We would have a GitHub organisation with a template repository; each participant gets a repository made from it, published with GitHub Pages. Everyone starts from the same base, and every app has a history.

  3. C

    A small team that moves them

    Participants publish from their own tool, share the link, and a team of the region's providers moves the app into the organisation for them during the afternoon. This is the first taste of villager services, on the day.

After the day

  • Every app has a page, with its owner's permission.
  • A follow-up within a week: what the app needs to be used by others, and who in the region can do it.

Many of these apps will break later, when more people need them and the person who built them has a day job. That is when the region's providers come in, and that is the market these sessions create.

The pack

A worked example, and everything in the open

The first example, the holiday home checklist, was converted into an app with the five prompts for the proposal. It shows the three phases, the essential steps first, a way to add a step, a note and a history for every run, and an honest "about" that says the data stays in the browser and that sharing between two phones is not there yet.

Open the worked example from the pack

The examples, the five prompts, the mock event plan and the converted app are published together as a public, read-only sgit vault (ph10xfc1), so our team can open it, copy it and use it for the first session. Everything in it is invented and licensed for reuse (CC BY 4.0).

What this does not claim

  • It is a proposal, with a mock event. No session has been run yet, and nothing in it is agreed.
  • Every example, person and figure in the pack is invented.
  • The converted app was built for the proposal, to show what the five prompts produce; a participant's will look different. So will the apps made by the tool on this page.
  • The models change. The prompts are written for any model that can build and publish a small web app.
